## Part 1 — Photophysics: What Actually Happens When UNH Fluoresces

### 1.1 The energy landscape: why we draw a Jablonski diagram at all

A molecule (or a molecular ion like uranyl, UO₂²⁺) has a manifold of electronic states. Each electronic state is itself split into vibrational sublevels (because the nuclei can vibrate against each other within that electronic configuration), and each vibrational level is split further into rotational sublevels (free rotation of the whole molecule). Fig. 1 in the manual is a **Jablonski diagram**: a bookkeeping device that shows every state as a horizontal line (grouped into columns by *spin multiplicity*, S = singlet, T = triplet) and every process as an arrow between lines.

The reason spin multiplicity gets its own axis is that transitions *within* a spin manifold (S₀→S₁→S₂…) are far easier than transitions *between* manifolds (S₁→T₁). This single fact — how electron spin couples to the transition — is what generates almost all of the enormous range of timescales you see in section II of the manual (10⁻¹⁵ s up to 10 s, eighteen orders of magnitude). Understanding *why* spin does this is the key to answering "why do some transitions take so much longer than others."

### 1.2 Absorption (A: S₀ + hν → Sₙ, ~10⁻¹⁵ s)

Absorption is the fastest process in the diagram because it isn't really a "process" with a rate in the usual kinetic sense — it's the response time of the electron cloud to an oscillating electromagnetic field. The electric field of a photon of visible/UV light oscillates with a period of order 10⁻¹⁵–10⁻¹⁶ s, and the electron redistributes (its wavefunction changes) on essentially that same timescale, because electrons are ~10⁴ times lighter than nuclei and respond almost inertia-free to the field. This ~10⁻¹⁵ s is not a decay/relaxation lifetime — it is closer to an oscillation period, i.e., the time for one cycle of the exciting light. Practically, you can treat absorption as instantaneous compared to everything else that follows.

**Why "vertical" transitions?** Because the nuclei are ~1800–200,000× heavier than electrons (mass of U ≈ 238 amu, O ≈ 16 amu, versus the electron), they simply cannot move on the ~10⁻¹⁵ s timescale of the electronic transition. This is the **Franck–Condon principle**: during the electronic transition, the nuclear positions and momenta are essentially frozen, so on an energy-vs-nuclear-coordinate diagram the transition is drawn as a *vertical* line — you go straight up from the ground electronic state's potential curve to the excited electronic state's potential curve, landing on whatever vibrational level of the excited state has a nuclear geometry (and the associated vibrational wavefunction overlap) closest to where you started. This is why absorption/emission spectra show *vibrational progressions* rather than single lines — you can land on v' = 0, 1, 2… of the excited state depending on how well each vibrational wavefunction overlaps the ground-state geometry (the **Franck–Condon factor**, |⟨v'|v⟩|²).

### 1.3 Internal conversion within Sₙ → S₁ (B: 10⁻¹¹–10⁻¹⁴ s)

If the UV photon has enough energy to reach S₂, S₃, S₄… (an "Sₙ" state, n>1), the molecule almost always drops non-radiatively down to S₁ before doing anything else. This is **internal conversion (IC)**: a *radiationless, spin-allowed* transition between states of the same multiplicity, where electronic energy is converted directly into vibrational energy of the same or a lower electronic state (the excess energy doesn't leave as a photon — it's dumped into nuclear motion, i.e., heat).

Why so fast (10⁻¹¹–10⁻¹⁴ s) and why does it always end at S₁ specifically? Two reasons combine into what's called **Kasha's rule** ("fluorescence normally occurs from S₁ regardless of which Sₙ was initially populated"):

- **Density of vibronic states.** The energy gaps between S₂, S�3, S4… are typically small compared to the gap between S₁ and S₀. A small energy gap means there are many nearly-isoenergetic vibrational levels of the lower electronic state available to receive the electronic energy — internal conversion is a resonance process (energy must be conserved), and a high density of accepting states makes the transition very fast (this "energy-gap law" — rate falls off exponentially as the gap widens — is a general feature of radiationless transitions).
- **Vibrational relaxation is even faster.** Even the small amount of "extra" vibrational energy dumped by IC is then shed to the surrounding lattice/solvent by vibrational relaxation (essentially molecular collisions / phonon coupling) on a similarly fast timescale, so population piles up and gets trapped in the lowest vibrational level of S₁ before the (much slower) S₁→S₀ radiative or intersystem-crossing processes can compete.

Between S₁ and S₀, however, the energy gap is usually large (this is the highest-energy gap in the whole singlet manifold), so IC directly from S₁ to S₀ (process **C(c)**, 10⁻⁵–10⁻⁷ s) is comparatively slow — slow enough that fluorescence (C(a)) and intersystem crossing (C(b)) can compete with it.

### 1.4 Why fluorescence (C(a): S₁ → S₀ + hν, 10⁻⁴–10⁻⁶ s) is "slow" compared to absorption but the fastest *radiative* process from S₁

Fluorescence is radiative and spin-allowed (S₁→S₀ conserves total spin, both singlets). Its rate is governed by the **transition dipole moment** between S₁ and S₀ via Fermi's Golden Rule / the Einstein A-coefficient:

$$k_r \;\propto\; \nu^3 \,|\mu_{10}|^2$$

where μ₁₀ is the transition dipole matrix element and ν is the transition frequency. This is a spontaneous-emission rate, and even for a fully allowed transition it's intrinsically much slower than absorption because absorption's "rate" is set by the driving field, whereas emission is a spontaneous quantum process whose rate is set by the coupling of the excited state to the vacuum electromagnetic field — a coupling that is weak compared to the ~10⁻¹⁵ s response of bound electrons to a *resonant, intense* driving field. Typical allowed electronic transition dipole moments give natural radiative lifetimes in the nanosecond range (10⁻⁹–10⁻⁶ s), consistent with the 10⁻⁴–10⁻⁶ s quoted (the manual's upper end, 10⁻⁴ s, reflects that uranyl's S₁→S₀ transition is only weakly allowed — see §1.7).

### 1.5 Intersystem crossing (C(b): S₁ → Tₙ, ~10⁻⁸ s) — why "forbidden" transitions still happen, just slower

Intersystem crossing (ISC) converts a singlet excited state into a triplet excited state. In the absence of any spin–orbit interaction, this transition is *rigorously forbidden* — the total spin operator commutes with a spin-independent, non-relativistic Hamiltonian, so pure spin-singlet and spin-triplet states cannot mix, and an electric-dipole transition cannot flip an electron's spin. ISC only happens because of **spin–orbit coupling (SOC)**: relativistic coupling between an electron's spin and its orbital angular momentum, which slightly mixes some triplet character into a nominally singlet state (and vice versa) — the electron doesn't so much "flip its spin" via the photon field as the *molecular eigenstate itself* is already a weakly-mixed spin state (§1.7 below explains why this is unusually strong here).

The rate of ISC scales with the square of the spin–orbit coupling matrix element, and SOC strength scales roughly as Z⁴ (Z = atomic number) — this is why ISC is negligible in light organic molecules (C, H, N, O; heaviest Z ≈ 8) but can dominate the excited-state photophysics of heavy-atom-containing species. This directly matters for **uranium** (Z = 92): the huge nuclear charge on the uranium atom in uranyl gives it strong intrinsic SOC ("heavy-atom effect"), which is a major reason uranyl compounds show efficient, long-lived, highly structured luminescence in the first place.

### 1.6 Phosphorescence (D(a): T₁ → S₀ + hν, 10⁻³–10 s) — the slowest radiative process, and why

Phosphorescence is radiative *and* spin-forbidden (T₁→S₀ requires a spin flip). Its rate is governed by the same Fermi's-Golden-Rule expression as fluorescence, but the effective transition dipole moment is not the "clean" one between pure singlet and pure triplet (which is exactly zero) — it's the dipole moment between the *spin–orbit-mixed* states, which is small precisely because the singlet admixture into the nominally-triplet T₁ state is small. Because rate ∝ |μ|² and |μ| itself is already suppressed by (typically) several orders of magnitude relative to an allowed transition, phosphorescence radiative rates end up 3–9 orders of magnitude slower than fluorescence — hence lifetimes from milliseconds to seconds (and, in especially spin-orbit-poor systems, minutes). This is also why phosphorescence is so easily quenched by collisions/oxygen in solution (there's plenty of time for a competing non-radiative or bimolecular process to intervene before the photon is finally emitted) and is generally only cleanly observed in rigid media (glasses, solids) at low temperature — which is part of the reason this experiment studies UNH as a **solid** (see §2.2).

### 1.7 Why radiative vs non-radiative, and why uranyl specifically is a good fluorophore for this experiment

Whether a given S₁-state molecule decays radiatively or non-radiatively is a *kinetic competition*: several parallel pathways (fluorescence, IC to S₀, ISC to Tₙ) are all draining the same S₁ population simultaneously, each with its own rate constant $k_i$, and the **branching fraction (quantum yield)** into channel $i$ is

$$\Phi_i = \frac{k_i}{\sum_j k_j}.$$

- A transition is **radiative** when a real photon must be emitted to conserve energy while the system returns to a lower-energy electronic configuration, and when the transition dipole moment coupling the two states is non-zero (governed by selection rules: parity, spin, orbital angular momentum).
- A transition is **non-radiative** when the electronic energy gap can instead be absorbed by nuclear vibrations (phonons in the lattice, or high-frequency vibrational modes of the molecule/ion itself) — i.e., when there's a good vibrational "ladder" (accepting modes with near-resonant vibrational quanta) to carry away the energy as heat instead of light. This is exactly the energy-gap-law argument from §1.3: small gaps favor non-radiative decay, large gaps favor radiative decay (because very high-order/high-energy vibrational overtones are needed to bridge a large gap non-radiatively, and their Franck–Condon factors fall off exponentially).

Uranyl (UO₂²⁺) fluoresces efficiently in the solid hexahydrate for a combination of reasons directly relevant to why this experiment is even feasible:
- The O=U=O emitting unit is embedded in a **rigid crystal lattice**. Rigidity suppresses large-amplitude vibrational and rotational motion that would otherwise open up fast non-radiative relaxation channels (this is the general principle behind why phosphors/fluorophores are often studied as crystals or in frozen glasses — compare to free molecules in solution or gas, which have many more low-frequency motional pathways for non-radiative loss).
- The relevant excited state in uranyl is actually derived from a formally spin/parity forbidden **ligand-to-metal charge-transfer** transition (an electron partially moves from the oxo-ligand orbitals onto uranium 5f orbitals) which is only weakly allowed — this is consistent with the *unusually long* fluorescence lifetime uranyl salts show (microseconds, at the long end of the "fluorescence" timescale bracket the manual gives, 10⁻⁴ s, rather than the nanosecond lifetimes typical of fully-allowed organic dye fluorescence) and gives sharp, well-resolved vibronic structure because the excited-state geometry doesn't change wildly from the ground state.
- Heavy-atom-enhanced spin–orbit coupling (§1.5) partially relaxes the formal spin-forbiddenness of this transition, which is *why it's bright enough to see at all* — a purely spin-forbidden transition with no heavy-atom help would be far too weak to observe on a simple nano-ammeter.

## Part 2 — Decoding the Equations on Page 3

### 2.1 Why total energy separates as $E_T = E_e + E_v + E_r$ (Eq. 1) — the Born–Oppenheimer approximation

This decomposition is not obvious from first principles — the full molecular Hamiltonian has electrons and nuclei all coupled together through Coulomb interactions. It becomes possible because of the same electron/nucleus mass disparity discussed in §1.2: electrons move so much faster than nuclei that, to very good approximation, the electrons instantaneously "relax" into their ground configuration for *any* fixed nuclear geometry. This is the **Born–Oppenheimer approximation**. It lets you:

1. First solve the electronic Schrödinger equation with the nuclei clamped at fixed positions, getting an electronic energy $E_e(R)$ that depends parametrically on the nuclear geometry $R$ — this $E_e(R)$ curve (or surface) is the "potential energy curve" nuclei then move on.
2. Then solve a *separate* Schrödinger equation for the nuclei moving on that potential curve, which itself separates (for a diatomic-like unit, or the collinear O=U=O unit treated as quasi-diatomic in this experiment) into a vibrational part (motion along the bond) and a rotational part (tumbling of the whole molecular axis in space).

Each of these three problems has its own quantized energy ladder — $T_e$ (electronic term value, the minimum of the potential curve), $G(v)$ (vibrational term value), $F(J)$ (rotational term value) — and because the approximation treats them as independent, the total energy is just their sum (Eq. 2). This is why spectroscopists universally use "term value" notation ($T$, in units of cm⁻¹, i.e., energy divided by $hc$) instead of raw energy — term values add and subtract like the terms in Eq. 4–6, which is very convenient for reading transition energies directly off a spectrum.

### 2.2 Why rotational energy is dropped for solid-state spectra (Eq. 3)

Rotational energy levels only exist as a well-defined, discrete quantum ladder if the molecule (or ion) is free to tumble in three-dimensional space — this is true for gas-phase molecules and reasonably true for small molecules tumbling in a low-viscosity liquid. In this experiment, however, the sample is **solid uranyl nitrate hexahydrate**, a crystalline material. In a crystal, the uranyl ion sits in a fixed lattice site, held by the surrounding nitrate and water ligands and neighboring ions; it cannot freely rotate — at most it can *librate* (small-amplitude rocking/torsional oscillation about its equilibrium orientation), which is really just another (low-frequency) vibrational-type mode, not free rotation.

Two consequences follow, and both justify Eq. 3:

- Since there's no free rotor, there's no rotational term $F(J)$ to speak of — the rotational "levels" don't exist as a separate quantized ladder in the solid; whatever their analog is, it's absorbed into low-frequency lattice/librational vibrational structure.
- Even if you wanted to imagine residual rotational-like structure, the individual rotational lines (each only fractions of a cm⁻¹ to a few cm⁻¹ apart in a free small molecule) would be completely washed out by **inhomogeneous broadening** in the solid: every uranyl ion in the crystal sits in a slightly different local strain/field environment, every unit cell has thermal disorder, and the electronic and vibrational transitions themselves already have linewidths of many cm⁻¹ from phonon coupling. A monochromator with a slit width of even 0.5 mm (a deliberately *coarse* setting — see §3.6) could never resolve rotational fine structure in a solid even if it existed. So neglecting $F(J)$ isn't just a convenient approximation — it's a statement that this level of structure is physically unobservable in this measurement, and Eq. 3 correctly reflects that only $T_e$ and $G(v)$ are meaningful, resolvable degrees of freedom here.

### 2.3 The transition-frequency expansion (Eq. 4) and what each symbol means

$$\omega = T' - T = (T_e' - T_e) + [G(v') - G(v)]$$

This is just Eq. 3 applied to both the excited state (primed) and ground state (unprimed), then subtracted, exactly as you'd read a transition energy off any spectroscopic term diagram: the observed photon energy (in wavenumbers, cm⁻¹, hence the symbol ω rather than a frequency in Hz — cm⁻¹ is the standard spectroscopic unit, $\tilde\nu = 1/\lambda$) equals the *difference in electronic origins* ($T_e' - T_e$, the pure electronic energy gap with both species in their v=0 vibrational levels) plus the *difference in vibrational term values* between whatever vibrational level v' you land in upstairs and whatever level v you started from downstairs.

The vibrational term value itself, for an anharmonic (real) oscillator, is conventionally written as a power series in the vibrational quantum number:

$$G(v) = \omega_e\left(v+\tfrac12\right) - \omega_e\chi_e\left(v+\tfrac12\right)^2 + \dots$$

Substituting this expansion (for both primed/excited and unprimed/ground states) into $\omega = (T_e'-T_e) + [G(v')-G(v)]$ produces exactly the messy expression the manual labels Eq. 4. It looks complicated only because it is the fully general expansion, retaining anharmonicity terms in *both* electronic states.

### 2.4 What "anharmonicity" physically means, and why $\chi_e, \chi_e'$ can be dropped (Eq. 5)

A chemical bond is *not* a perfect spring. The simplest model of a vibrating bond is the **harmonic oscillator**: a parabolic potential energy curve, $V(x) = \tfrac12 k x^2$, for which the quantized energy levels are exactly evenly spaced ($G(v) = \omega_e(v+\tfrac12)$, spacing exactly $\omega_e$ between any two adjacent levels, all the way up). Real bonds, however, follow something closer to a **Morse potential**: steep on the compressed side (very hard to push atoms closer than their equilibrium separation — strong short-range repulsion) but shallow and eventually flat on the stretched side (the bond weakens and eventually dissociates as you pull the atoms apart — there's a finite dissociation energy, not an infinite parabolic wall). This asymmetry is "anharmonicity," and it has a direct, testable consequence: **the energy-level spacing shrinks slightly as v increases** — levels crowd together near the dissociation limit instead of staying evenly spaced forever. The correction term $-\omega_e\chi_e(v+\tfrac12)^2$ captures exactly this: $\chi_e$ (the "anharmonicity constant") is a small, positive, dimensionless-ish correction factor (typically $\chi_e \ll 1$, often ~0.001–0.01 relative to $\omega_e$) that makes level spacing decrease slightly with increasing v.

You are told to **neglect** $\chi_e$ and $\chi_e'$ (Eq. 5) for two compounding reasons:
1. **You are only probing very low vibrational quantum numbers.** The manual has you look at the (0-0), (0-1), (0-2), (0-3) transitions (Fig. 2) — i.e., emission always originates from v'=0 of the excited state and lands on v = 0, 1, 2, 3 of the *ground* state. Near the bottom of a potential well, the true (Morse) potential is still extremely well approximated by a parabola — anharmonicity is a large-v effect (it matters most near dissociation, far from where you're measuring), so at small v the harmonic approximation is already quite accurate.
2. **The anharmonic correction term is quadratic in $(v+\tfrac12)$ while the harmonic term is linear**, and $\chi_e$ itself is small — so for v = 0,1,2,3 the anharmonic term is a small correction on top of an already small quantity, doubly suppressed. Dropping it introduces an error much smaller than other sources of uncertainty in this experiment (slit-width-limited resolution, drum-reading precision — see Part 4), so it isn't worth carrying through the algebra.

Dropping the $\chi_e(v+\tfrac12)^2$ terms from the full expansion in Eq. 4 collapses it to the purely linear (harmonic) form:

$$\omega = \omega_0 + \omega_e' v' - \omega_e v \qquad \text{(Eq. 5)}$$

where $\omega_0 \equiv (T_e' - T_e) + \tfrac12\omega_e' - \tfrac12\omega_e$ absorbs all the v-independent constants (including the zero-point-energy difference between the two electronic states) into a single "band origin" frequency.

### 2.5 Why $v' = 0$ is assumed (Eq. 6), and what that says about the physics

$$\omega = \omega_0 - \omega_e v \qquad \text{(Eq. 6)}$$

Setting v' = 0 in Eq. 5 is not an arbitrary simplification — it is a direct experimental fact enforced by **Kasha's rule and fast vibrational relaxation**, the same physics from §1.3. Whatever excited vibronic level the 365 nm excitation photon initially populates, vibrational relaxation *within* the excited electronic state (collisional/phonon-mediated energy loss to the lattice) is extremely fast (picoseconds) compared to the fluorescence lifetime (microseconds, per §1.7). So essentially 100% of the population that is about to fluoresce has already relaxed down to v' = 0 of S₁ *before* it emits. This means every fluorescence photon originates from the same starting point (v'=0), and the *only* thing that varies from one emission line to the next is which ground-state vibrational level (v = 0, 1, 2, 3, …) it lands on — exactly the (0-0), (0-1), (0-2), (0-3) progression drawn in Fig. 2. This is also *why* the emission spectrum is a clean, evenly-spaced-ish progression of bands rather than a continuum or a chaotic set of lines: you're reading off the ground-state vibrational ladder, one quantum at a time, all referenced to the same upper-state origin.

From Eq. 6, note that as v increases by 1 (going from the (0-0) band to (0-1), (0-1) to (0-2), etc.), $\omega$ *decreases* by exactly $\omega_e$ each time (lower photon energy = longer wavelength, i.e., each successive fluorescence band is red-shifted from the previous one by a fixed amount) — **this is exactly why the successive-band-spacing method for extracting $\omega_e$ works**, and why $\omega_e$ is described as "the difference (in cm⁻¹) between successive bands" (§4.3 below shows how to use this in practice).

### 2.6 The force-constant equation (Eq. 7) — where it comes from and what "reduced mass of the oxygen atom" means here

$$\omega_e = \frac{1}{2\pi c}\left(\frac{f}{m_o}\right)^{1/2}$$

This is the standard harmonic-oscillator vibrational-frequency formula, $\omega_e[\text{cm}^{-1}] = \frac{1}{2\pi c}\sqrt{k/\mu}$, applied to the **symmetric stretch of the linear O=U=O uranyl unit** rather than to a simple diatomic. In an ordinary diatomic AB, $\mu$ is the standard two-body reduced mass $\mu = m_A m_B/(m_A+m_B)$. Uranyl is a **linear symmetric triatomic** (O=U=O, like CO₂), so its three normal modes are the symmetric stretch, the antisymmetric stretch, and a doubly-degenerate bend — the fluorescence progression observed here tracks the **totally symmetric stretch**, in which both oxygen atoms move outward (or inward) in phase while the (much heavier) central uranium atom stays essentially stationary by symmetry (its center-of-mass constraint requires zero net momentum, and since the two oxygen displacements are equal and opposite in this mode relative to U, and U is ~15× heavier than O, U's own displacement is small). Because uranium barely moves in this particular mode, the effective reduced mass that governs the symmetric-stretch frequency reduces (under the "valence force field" approximation the manual mentions — a model where only U–O bond-stretching force constants matter, not the U mass or angular deformation terms) to essentially just $m_O$, the mass of a single oxygen atom — which is why the formula uses "the reduced mass of the oxygen atom" rather than a full three-body reduced mass. This is stated as following from "collinearity of structure" (the O=U=O unit is linear, so the two O–U bonds stretch along the same axis and the symmetric combination has this clean approximate decoupling from the uranium mass) and "applicability of the valence force field" (bonding described purely by bond-stretch force constants $f$, ignoring cross-terms/bending coupling).

The unit-conversion form given,

$$f = 5.8883\times 10^{-2}\, m_o\, \omega_e^{1/2}\ \text{dyne/cm}$$

is simply Eq. 7 algebraically rearranged to solve for $f$ instead of $\omega_e$, with the numerical prefactor absorbing the constants $(2\pi c)^2$ *and* the necessary unit conversions to go from atomic mass units (the "Aston scale," i.e., $m_O \approx 16$ in amu) and cm⁻¹ to force in dyne/cm (CGS units) — you should **not** derive this prefactor from scratch; just plug in $\omega_e$ (from your data, in cm⁻¹) and $m_o = 16$ (amu) directly into this rearranged formula to get $f$ in dyne/cm.

**Practical takeaway for your calculation:** measure $\omega_e$ from the spacing between your fluorescence bands (§4.3), plug it into the boxed formula above with $m_o = 16$, and you have the U–O bond force constant — this is objective (iv).

## Part 3 — The Experimental Setup: Design Choices Explained

### 3.1 Overall logic of the two-stage procedure

The experiment has exactly two measurement stages because you need two independent things from the monochromator, and it cannot give you both at once:

1. **Stage 1 (fluorescence run):** record signal (galvanometer/nA-meter deflection) vs. **drum reading** (an arbitrary mechanical dial number — it has no physical unit of wavelength printed on it) as you rotate the dispersing prism. This tells you *where the peaks are* in drum-reading space, but not yet in wavelength space, because the drum reading vs. wavelength relationship for a **prism** instrument is nonlinear (§3.5) and is not something you can compute from first principles without instrument-specific data.
2. **Stage 2 (calibration run):** replace the unknown fluorescence source with a **known** source (a mercury vapor lamp, whose emission wavelengths are tabulated to great precision — Table I) and again record signal vs. drum reading. Because you now know the *true* wavelength of each peak you observe (from Table I), you can build a **drum-reading ↔ wavelength lookup/calibration curve**, and then use *that same curve* to convert your Stage-1 drum readings (whose wavelengths you don't otherwise know) into real wavelengths.

This two-stage logic — measure the unknown, then immediately calibrate the same instrument against a known standard, under the same optical/mechanical conditions — is the standard way to use any dispersive instrument whose dial isn't already a directly-trustworthy wavelength readout, and is why objective (i) ("calibration... using a mercury source") is listed as a distinct objective from (ii) ("recording... fluorescence spectrum").

### 3.2 Why a mercury vapor lamp is the calibration standard

A mercury discharge lamp is used because mercury's atomic emission lines are extremely well characterized, narrow (true atomic lines, not broad molecular bands), and reproducible — the wavelengths in Table I (404.7, 435.8, 546.1 nm, etc.) are fixed physical constants of the mercury atom (transitions between specific electronic energy levels of Hg), not something that depends on your particular lamp or drifts with time/temperature. This makes Hg lines an ideal, universally-reproducible "ruler" for wavelength calibration — essentially every spectroscopy lab uses a mercury (or mercury-argon) lamp for exactly this purpose, because you don't need any other instrument to know these wavelengths — they're tabulated once and used everywhere.

### 3.3 The 365 nm U-V excitation lamp: why UV, and why specifically ~365 nm

You need a photon energetic enough to promote the uranyl ion from its ground electronic state (S₀) up to an excited electronic state (S₁ or higher) — i.e., the excitation photon energy must match (or exceed) the electronic absorption gap, which for uranyl's ligand-to-metal charge-transfer absorption band lies in the near-UV. A photon at 365 nm carries roughly 3.4 eV of energy, which is well-matched to uranyl's absorption band (uranyl salts are in fact used as classic actinometers/UV indicators precisely because they absorb efficiently in the UV-A range and re-emit visible green fluorescence — the ~365 nm mercury line is one of the strongest, most standard emission lines of a simple mercury-vapor UV lamp, which is why it's the conventional choice for a benchtop excitation source rather than a specialized laser). Practically: this is *also just a mercury line* (mercury has a strong emission line very close to 365 nm), so the "U-V lamp" in this experiment is really the same type of source as the mercury calibration lamp, just isolated to (or dominated by) its UV output using a filter, rather than the full visible-line spectrum used in Table I. (Mercury emits at both 365 nm in the UV and at the visible lines in Table I — a single mercury-type source, or a dedicated UV mercury lamp, is standard because it's inexpensive, robust, and gives a strong, well-defined excitation wavelength without needing a tunable laser.)

### 3.4 Why a metallic parabolic reflector, not a lens, to focus the excitation light

The parabolic reflector sits in front of the U-V lamp and focuses its (divergent, roughly point-source) UV output onto the sample. Two independent reasons favor a **metallic, front-surface, parabolic mirror** over a refractive lens for this job:

- **No chromatic aberration.** A lens focuses light by refraction, and a material's refractive index depends on wavelength (dispersion) — so a simple glass/quartz lens would focus different wavelengths at slightly different points, degrading how tightly the UV light is focused onto the sample and wasting excitation intensity. A mirror focuses light by reflection, and the law of reflection has *no* wavelength dependence — a parabolic mirror focuses a beam parallel to its axis to a single point *perfectly, for any wavelength simultaneously* (that's the defining geometric property of a parabola: it converts a point source at the focus into a perfectly collimated/re-focused beam, exactly, independent of color). This matters because the lamp's real spectral output isn't a single perfect line, and even the 365 nm "line" has finite width.
- **UV transmission losses.** Ordinary optical glass absorbs UV light strongly below ~350 nm (this is why UV-specific optics use fused silica/quartz, which is more expensive) — a metallic mirror simply reflects the UV rather than having to transmit through bulk material, sidestepping this materials problem entirely and giving high reflectivity (and hence high collection/focusing efficiency) across UV and visible wavelengths without needing a specialty UV-grade lens material.

A parabolic shape specifically (rather than, say, spherical) is used because it is *free of spherical aberration on-axis* — every ray from a point source at the exact focus reflects to become perfectly parallel (or vice-versa, parallel rays are all focused to the exact same point), which a spherical mirror only does approximately, and only for rays close to the optical axis. This gives a tighter, more efficient focus of the lamp's output onto the small sample area, which matters because fluorescence intensity from the sample scales with how much excitation light you can actually concentrate on it.

### 3.5 Why the monochromator uses a prism, and how a prism monochromator actually works (Fig. 4)

**Function of a monochromator in general:** it takes light containing many wavelengths (either the polychromatic fluorescence emitted by the sample, or the multi-line mercury calibration spectrum) and disperses it in space by wavelength, so that only a narrow wavelength band passes through a fixed exit slit at any one drum-angle setting — by mechanically rotating the dispersing element (here, the prism), you scan which wavelength band reaches the exit slit and hence the detector.

**Reading Fig. 4 (the ray diagram):** light enters at the entrance slit (1), diverges, and hits a concave mirror (2) which collimates it into a parallel beam. That parallel beam passes through the dispersing prism (4), which refracts different wavelengths by different amounts (this differential refraction — dispersion — is the entire point of the prism: it is what physically separates the wavelengths in angle). The dispersed, still-parallel beams (now at slightly different angles per wavelength) are re-focused by a second concave mirror (5) onto the exit slit (6), where only the wavelength (or narrow band) whose angle happens to line up with the exit-slit position actually passes through; everything else is blocked by the housing. Rotating the prism (via the "drum") changes which wavelength satisfies this geometric condition and therefore exits — that's the whole mechanism of wavelength selection. (Item 3, a plane mirror, simply folds the optical path within the compact housing; it doesn't disperse or focus, it just redirects the beam.)

**Why a prism specifically, and why it matters for §3.5-onward analysis (Q3 in the manual):** a prism disperses light by *refraction*, and the refractive index $n(\lambda)$ of the prism glass follows a smooth but strongly **nonlinear** function of wavelength — typically well-described (away from absorption bands) by something like the Cauchy relation, $n(\lambda) \approx A + B/\lambda^2$, meaning the prism bends blue/UV light much more strongly than red light (dispersion is highest at short wavelengths, and drops off — becomes much flatter — toward longer/red wavelengths). Since the drum angle needed to bring a given wavelength to the exit slit is directly tied to the prism's refraction angle at that wavelength, **the relationship between drum reading and wavelength is correspondingly nonlinear** — it is *not* a straight line, and equal steps in drum reading do *not* correspond to equal steps in wavelength (you get finer wavelength resolution, more nm-per-drum-division, at the red end, and coarser resolution at the blue end, for a given angular step). This is precisely why the manual has you build an empirical calibration *curve* (not just a two-point linear scale) from several mercury lines spanning the visible range — you need enough calibration points to trace out this nonlinear mapping accurately (see §4.2).

By contrast, a **diffraction grating** disperses light by interference rather than refraction, and to first (and often very good) approximation the angular dispersion of a grating is *linear* in wavelength (from the grating equation $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$, for fixed order $m$ and small angular range, $\theta$ varies roughly linearly with $\lambda$) — so a grating-based monochromator's drum-reading-vs-wavelength relationship would be far closer to a straight line than this prism instrument's. This is the direct answer to the manual's Q3 ("would it be the same or different if we were to use a grating"): **different — and specifically, more linear** with a grating.

### 3.6 Why 90° collection geometry for the fluorescence signal

The lens assembly collects the sample's fluorescence and focuses it onto the monochromator's entrance slit at **90° to the excitation beam axis**, rather than collecting fluorescence that is co-linear with (in front of or behind) the exciting UV beam. The reason is signal-to-noise: the excitation source is enormously more intense than the (weak) fluorescence it produces, so if you tried to collect fluorescence *along* the excitation beam axis, you would also collect a huge amount of directly transmitted and/or specularly/diffusely scattered excitation light straight into your detector — this stray excitation light would swamp the much weaker fluorescence signal and (via elastic scattering, i.e., light at the *same* wavelength as the exciting 365 nm, not the red/green-shifted fluorescence) could not be distinguished from real signal by wavelength alone at the monochromator. Viewing the sample from **90° to the excitation beam** geometrically minimizes the amount of directly-scattered excitation light that can reach the entrance slit along that path (scattered/reflected excitation light preferentially continues in directions close to the original beam direction — forward and backward — rather than sideways), giving a much cleaner background for detecting the weaker, sideways-radiating fluorescence signal. This 90°-geometry is completely standard in fluorimetry for exactly this reason (it is used in essentially all commercial fluorimeters).

**Sample optics material:** because you are working with a UV excitation beam (365 nm) that must reach the sample, and green/visible fluorescence that must be collected and transmitted through a lens, the *lens* in the 90° collection assembly (and any windows the light passes through) should be made of a UV-transmitting material such as fused quartz/fused silica rather than ordinary optical glass — ordinary glass strongly absorbs below ~350 nm and would attenuate stray/leaked UV excessively and, more importantly, could itself fluoresce weakly under UV excitation, adding unwanted background signal.

### 3.7 The photomultiplier tube (PMT): why it's needed, and what the "maximum voltage" limits mean

Fluorescence from a small solid sample, further attenuated by passing through a slit-limited monochromator, is a genuinely weak optical signal — far too weak for an ordinary photodiode or photoresistor to register a usable current. A **PMT** converts each incoming photon into a photoelectron at a photocathode (via the photoelectric effect), then accelerates and multiplies that single electron through a chain of secondary-emission stages called **dynodes**, each held at a progressively higher voltage than the last (supplied by the HV(0–1.2 kV) DC unit in Fig. 3). At each dynode, one incoming electron knocks loose several secondary electrons, and this cascades multiplicatively through typically 8–14 dynode stages — giving overall current gains of 10⁵–10⁸ **electrons out per photon in**. This is what turns an otherwise undetectably faint fluorescence signal into a measurable current at the nano-ampere meter/galvanometer.

- **Gain depends steeply on the applied HV** (gain typically scales as roughly $V^{kn}$, where $n$ is the number of dynode stages — a strongly super-linear dependence), which is exactly why the manual has you *reduce the PMT voltage*, not the slit width, to bring an overly bright signal (the intense mercury calibration lines) back into the meter's readable range in Stage 2 (§3.1's Precaution 2/Procedure step 2): reducing slit width would change the instrument's resolution and throughput geometry (undesirable — you want the same optical/mechanical conditions for calibration as for the actual measurement, to the extent practical), whereas the PMT voltage is purely an electronic gain knob that doesn't touch the optical path at all.
- **Why the manual caps PMT voltage far below its rated maximum** (650 V for fluorescence, only 300 V for the much-brighter mercury calibration line, versus a power-supply capability of up to 1200 V, with an absolute safety ceiling of 700 V stated in the precautions): PMTs are delicate, sensitive detectors that can be **permanently damaged by excessive current** — if you apply high gain (high voltage) while also exposing the tube to bright light, the resulting current through the dynode chain can exceed the tube's rated maximum, degrading or destroying the photocathode/dynode coatings. This is also why Precaution 3 tells you to **never leave PMT voltage on when not in use**, and Precaution 2 tells you to **prevent all stray light from reaching the PMT** — the device is optimized for detecting very *low* light levels with very high gain, which makes it correspondingly vulnerable to bright/stray light.

### 3.8 Slit widths: the resolution/throughput trade-off, and why coarse-then-fine

The manual specifies entrance and exit slits kept under 2 mm for "coarse alignment," then narrowed to 0.5 mm "for better resolution" — never closed fully. This reflects a fundamental trade-off in every dispersive spectrometer:

- **Narrower slits → better spectral resolution.** The exit slit only passes a small angular (and hence wavelength) range of dispersed light at each drum setting; the narrower that slit, the narrower the band of wavelengths that reaches the detector at once, so closely-spaced spectral features (like the two nearby yellow-orange mercury lines at 577.0 and 579.1 nm in Table I) can be told apart rather than blurring into a single unresolved peak. In the limit of an infinitely narrow slit you'd have perfect (but practically unusable) resolution.
- **Narrower slits → less light throughput → weaker signal / worse signal-to-noise.** Optical throughput (étendue) scales with slit area, so a narrower slit passes proportionally less light. Since the fluorescence signal is already weak (§3.7), closing the slits too far starves the detector of signal, and you'd need to push the PMT gain (voltage) up to compensate — which reintroduces noise and risks tube damage.

Hence the two-step procedure: start wide (≤2 mm, generous throughput) to quickly find approximately where the peaks are ("coarse alignment," fast to scan, easy to see a strong signal even if the wavelength resolution is mediocre), then narrow to 0.5 mm once you know roughly where to look, trading some signal strength for the resolution needed to pin down peak *positions* precisely — never fully closed, because a fully closed slit passes zero light and gives no signal at all.

### 3.9 Backlash error and why you always scan in one direction

Precaution 6 ("always measure from higher drum readings to lower drum readings") and the procedure's note about "unidirectional rotation... to avoid backlash error" both address the same well-known mechanical issue in any gear/screw-driven instrument: **mechanical backlash** is the small amount of "play" or looseness between mating mechanical parts (gears, lead-screw and nut, etc.) that must be taken up before motion in a *new* direction actually translates into motion of the final component (here, the prism). If you scan the drum in one direction, then reverse briefly, then continue, a bit of that reversal motion goes into silently taking up the backlash rather than actually rotating the prism — so the drum-reading dial no longer accurately reflects the true prism angle at that instant, introducing a small, direction-dependent systematic error in your recorded drum reading vs. the true wavelength. Always approaching every reading from the *same* direction of rotation (e.g., always decreasing, per Precaution 6) ensures the backlash is taken up consistently the same way every time, eliminating this source of systematic error from your calibration and measurement data.

## Part 4 — Data Analysis Walkthrough

### 4.1 Step-by-step overview (mapping onto manual section V)

1. Plot Stage-1 raw data: **signal (nA/galvanometer deflection) vs. drum reading**, for the fluorescence emission scan.
2. Plot Stage-2 raw data: **signal vs. drum reading**, for the mercury calibration scan.
3. On the Stage-2 plot, identify each resolved peak and match it to its known wavelength from Table I (matching by relative intensity pattern as well as position — see §4.2).
4. Build a **calibration curve**: drum reading (x) vs. known wavelength (y), fit through the calibration points.
5. Use the calibration curve as a lookup function to convert every drum-reading value on the Stage-1 (fluorescence) plot into a wavelength, producing an **intensity vs. wavelength** fluorescence spectrum.
6. From this wavelength-axis fluorescence spectrum, identify the vibronic progression (the peaks corresponding to the (0-0), (0-1), (0-2), (0-3)… bands of Fig. 2), and extract $\omega_0$ and $\omega_e$ per §4.3, then $f$ from Eq. 7.

### 4.2 Building the calibration curve properly

Because the drum-reading/wavelength relationship is nonlinear for a prism instrument (§3.5), you should **not** fit a straight line through just two points. Instead:

- Use as many resolved mercury lines as you can clearly identify (ideally most of the eight rows of Table I, though the very weak 407.8 nm line and the two closely-spaced 577.0/579.1 nm lines may be hard to resolve individually at coarse slit settings — expect to comfortably resolve the brightest, most well-separated lines: 404.7, 435.8, 546.1 nm, and possibly the 577/579 doublet as a single broadened peak or two very close peaks depending on your slit width and resolution).
- Use the **relative intensities in Table I** as a sanity check when assigning peaks: the strongest line by far should be 435.8 nm (intensity 4000), a close second is 404.7 nm (1800), and 546.1 nm (1100) should also be prominent — if your strongest observed peak doesn't correspond to the drum reading you'd expect to be near 435.8 nm relative to the others, re-check your assignment before trusting it.
- Fit a smooth curve (a low-order polynomial, e.g. quadratic or cubic, in drum-reading vs. wavelength, is standard and physically reasonable given the smooth $n(\lambda)$ dependence discussed in §3.5) through your (drum reading, known λ) points, rather than connecting them with straight-line segments. This fitted curve, not the raw data points, is what you use to convert the fluorescence-run drum readings to wavelengths.
- Restrict your working range to roughly where the manual says visible light appears for this instrument's present alignment (drum readings ≈2400–2850, per Precaution 5) — don't extrapolate your calibration fit far outside the range spanned by your calibration points, since a polynomial fit can behave unpredictably (and inaccurately) outside the range it was actually constrained by data.

### 4.3 Extracting $\omega_0$ and $\omega_e$ from the fluorescence progression

Once you have the fluorescence spectrum on a wavelength axis, convert each identified band's wavelength $\lambda_i$ (in nm) to a wavenumber $\tilde\nu_i = 1/\lambda_i$ (customarily reported in cm⁻¹, so convert nm → cm first: $\tilde\nu[\text{cm}^{-1}] = 10^7/\lambda[\text{nm}]$).

From Eq. 6, $\omega = \omega_0 - \omega_e v$, with the bands you observe corresponding to $v = 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$ (the (0-0), (0-1), (0-2), (0-3) progression):

- The **(0-0) band itself directly gives you $\omega_0$** (set v=0 in Eq. 6: $\omega_{(0,0)} = \omega_0$). Identify the (0-0) band as the **highest-energy (shortest-wavelength) member of the progression** — it's the origin band, with no vibrational quantum deposited in the ground state, so it sits at the highest photon energy of the series (all subsequent bands are red-shifted from it by successive multiples of $\omega_e$, per Eq. 6).
- $\omega_e$ is obtained from the **spacing between consecutive bands**: $\omega_e = \omega_{(0,v)} - \omega_{(0,v+1)}$ for each adjacent pair. Since you should observe at least four bands (three independent spacings: (0-0)−(0-1), (0-1)−(0-2), (0-2)−(0-3)), compute all three spacings and **average them** (as the manual explicitly instructs — "take the difference between successive bands and average it out") rather than relying on a single pair. Averaging reduces the impact of any one peak-position misidentification and gives you both a best estimate of $\omega_e$ and, from the spread of the three individual spacing values, a sense of your measurement's internal consistency/precision (see §4.4).
- As a self-consistency check, once you have averaged $\omega_e$, you can predict where each band *should* fall via Eq. 6 and compare to your actual band positions — good agreement across all bands (not just adjacent pairs) is evidence that you've correctly identified the origin band and that Eq. 6 (with anharmonicity neglected) is a good description of your data in this v-range, consistent with the justification in §2.4.

### 4.4 Sources of error and how to think about them

Go through the measurement chain in order and identify where uncertainty enters at each stage; this is both good experimental practice and the basis for a defensible error-analysis section in your report.

**(a) Peak-position reading uncertainty (systematic + random).** Every drum reading you record for a peak (calibration line or fluorescence band) carries some uncertainty from (i) the finite width of the spectral feature itself convolved with your slit width — a peak isn't infinitely sharp, it has a lineshape with some width, and your slit width sets an instrumental resolution floor below which you cannot pin down the true peak center any more precisely (§3.8); and (ii) how precisely you, the observer, can read/interpolate the drum's mechanical dial and judge exactly where the signal maximum occurs (especially since you're manually taking discrete readings, not a continuous automated scan — the true peak could fall between two of your sampled drum positions). Taking several closely-spaced readings around each suspected peak (as instructed — "take readings within every measurable fraction of drum reading, especially near the peak of a band") and fitting a smooth curve to interpolate the true maximum, rather than just taking the single highest raw reading, reduces this source of error.

**(b) Mechanical backlash error.** Discussed in §3.9 — a systematic offset that appears if scan direction is not held consistent. Even with unidirectional scanning as instructed, residual backlash can still contribute some uncertainty at the level of the mechanism's play; this is best minimized by procedure (as instructed) rather than corrected for after the fact.

**(c) Calibration-curve fitting error.** The fitted drum-reading↔wavelength curve is only as good as (i) how many calibration points you have and how well they span your working range, and (ii) how well your chosen fit function (e.g., a particular polynomial order) actually represents the true, smooth but nonlinear $n(\lambda)$-driven relationship (§3.5) — too low an order under-fits real curvature (introducing systematic error especially near the ends of your range), too high an order can over-fit noise in your few calibration points. This uncertainty propagates directly into every wavelength you subsequently read off the fluorescence spectrum using this curve, so it's worth reporting a residual/goodness-of-fit measure (e.g., how far each calibration point falls from the fitted curve) as your estimate of calibration uncertainty.

**(d) PMT/detector noise and stray light.** The nano-ampere-level signals here are close to the noise floor of the detection electronics; dark current (thermal electron emission from the photocathode/dynodes even with no light present) and any stray/scattered room or excitation light reaching the PMT (Precaution 2) add a background offset and random fluctuation on top of the true fluorescence signal, which can shift where you judge a peak center to be, especially for weak bands (the higher-v bands in the fluorescence progression are typically weaker than the (0-0) band, per the Franck–Condon-factor argument in §1.2, and so are more vulnerable to this).

**(e) Signal-to-drum-reading non-simultaneity / instrument drift.** Because you take discrete point-by-point readings rather than a continuous trace, and the whole scan takes some real time, any slow drift in lamp intensity, PMT gain (e.g., from slight HV supply drift or warm-up effects), or ambient conditions between the start and end of a scan introduces a slow systematic trend across your dataset that could be mistaken for real spectral structure if not accounted for.

**(f) Propagation into $\omega_e$ and $f$.** Since $\omega_e$ is obtained as an *average of differences* between wavenumbers (each individually subject to (a)–(e) above), a standard-deviation or standard-error calculation across your (typically three) independent spacing estimates gives a reasonable *empirical* estimate of the uncertainty in $\omega_e$ (this is exactly what "average it out" — and reporting the spread around that average — is for). Since $f \propto \omega_e^{1/2}$ (rearranged Eq. 7 — note the **square-root**, not linear, dependence), the *relative* (fractional) uncertainty in $f$ is **half** the relative uncertainty in $\omega_e$: $\delta f/f \approx \tfrac12\, \delta\omega_e/\omega_e$ — worth stating explicitly in your report, since it shows the force-constant result is somewhat less sensitive to $\omega_e$'s uncertainty than a linear propagation would suggest.

## Part 5 — Direct Answers to the Manual's End-of-Experiment Questions

*(These pull together material from Parts 1–4; use them as a starting point and expand in your own words for the actual lab report.)*

**Q1. Why do we use a metallic parabolic reflector in this experiment?**
Two reasons combine: (a) reflective optics have no chromatic aberration, unlike a refractive lens, so all wavelengths in the (nominally 365 nm, but finite-width) excitation output are focused to the same point with equal efficiency (§3.4); (b) a metallic mirror avoids the UV-absorption losses that ordinary glass lenses suffer below ~350 nm, without needing a specialty UV-transmitting lens material. The *parabolic* shape specifically (rather than spherical) is chosen because it focuses a point source to a perfectly parallel beam (and vice versa) with no spherical aberration, giving the tightest, most efficient focus of the lamp's UV output onto the small sample.

**Q2. What is the source of generation of U-V light in the U-V lamp in this experiment?**
The lamp is a mercury-vapor discharge source: an electric discharge excites mercury atoms to higher electronic states, and one of the strong resulting emission lines of atomic mercury falls at ~365 nm (in the near-UV), which is used (isolated, e.g. by a filter, from mercury's other visible lines) as the excitation source. This is the same underlying physical process (atomic emission from excited-state mercury) that produces the visible calibration lines in Table I — it is simply a different characteristic wavelength of the same element's emission spectrum.

**Q3. Comment on the functional dependence of drum reading vs. wavelength. Would it be the same or different if we were to use a grating for dispersion?**
For this prism-based instrument, drum reading vs. wavelength is **nonlinear**, because the prism disperses light by refraction and the refractive index $n(\lambda)$ of the prism material is itself a nonlinear (roughly Cauchy-like, $n \approx A + B/\lambda^2$) function of wavelength — dispersion (and hence angular deflection per unit drum rotation) is much stronger at short (blue/UV) wavelengths than at long (red) wavelengths. This is why a smooth polynomial calibration curve (not a straight line) built from several known mercury lines is required (§3.5, §4.2). If a **diffraction grating** were used instead, the dispersion relationship (from the grating equation, $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$) is, to good approximation over a modest angular range, close to **linear** in wavelength — so a grating instrument's drum-reading-vs-wavelength curve would be noticeably more linear (flatter, more uniform nm-per-division) than this prism instrument's.

**Q4. What material is the optics made of? Why do we gather fluorescence radiation at 90° geometry?**
Because both UV excitation light (365 nm) and visible fluorescence must pass through the relevant lenses/optics, these components should be made of a UV-transmitting material such as fused quartz/fused silica rather than ordinary optical glass, which absorbs strongly below ~350 nm and can itself fluoresce under UV excitation, adding background (§3.6). The mirrors within the monochromator itself are front-surface metallic reflectors for the same broadband, non-absorbing reasons as the excitation-side parabolic reflector (§3.4). Fluorescence is collected at 90° to the excitation beam specifically to minimize how much directly-transmitted or specularly/diffusely scattered excitation light reaches the detection path — scattered excitation light strongly favors continuing near the original beam direction (forward/backward), so viewing from the side gives a much better signal-to-background ratio for the comparatively weak fluorescence signal (§3.6).

**Q5. How would the intensity of the fluorescence change if the temperature of the salt is increased or decreased?**
Fluorescence intensity (more precisely, fluorescence quantum yield) generally **decreases as temperature increases, and increases as temperature decreases**. Raising the temperature increases the thermal population and amplitude of lattice vibrations/phonons and of the uranyl ion's own vibrational and librational motion (§1.7, §3.9's discussion of rigidity); these motions open up and speed up non-radiative relaxation pathways (internal conversion, non-radiative coupling to the lattice — essentially thermally-activated "vibrational/phonon-assisted" quenching), which compete more effectively against the (comparatively slow, per §1.4/§1.7) radiative fluorescence pathway and reduce $\Phi_{fl} = k_r/(k_r + \sum k_{nr})$ by increasing $k_{nr}$. Conversely, cooling the sample suppresses these thermal/vibrational non-radiative channels (a more rigid, less thermally agitated lattice, as discussed in §1.7), so lower temperature typically increases fluorescence intensity/yield and can also sharpen the vibronic bands (less thermal/inhomogeneous broadening).

**Q6. If the sample UNH is dissolved in water, will it still produce the same fluorescence spectrum?**
Not identically — you'd expect the fluorescence to persist (uranyl fluoresces in aqueous solution too, and is in fact a classic actinometric/analytical fluorophore in solution as well as in the solid), but with noticeably different spectral characteristics for several physically distinct reasons: (i) in solution, the assumption behind Eq. 3 (that rotational energy can be neglected — §2.2) no longer clearly applies in the same way, and more importantly the sharp, well-resolved vibronic bands seen in the rigid crystal typically **broaden and can partially wash out** in solution, because dissolved uranyl ions are surrounded by a fluctuating, disordered solvation shell of water molecules undergoing rapid thermal motion — this introduces much more inhomogeneous and dynamic broadening than the fixed, ordered crystal lattice environment (directly connected to §1.7's point about rigidity suppressing non-radiative/broadening pathways); (ii) coordinating water molecules can directly perturb the uranyl ion's local ligand field and vibronic coupling relative to its specific solid-state coordination environment (in the crystal, uranyl is coordinated by nitrate and water ligands in a particular fixed geometry set by the crystal structure; in solution it's typically coordinated by a shell of water molecules in a dynamically averaged, generally different arrangement), which can shift band positions somewhat; (iii) solution-phase uranyl fluorescence is also well known to be quenched by dissolved species (notably halide ions, nitrate concentration effects, and dissolved oxygen), so overall intensity/yield may differ substantially depending on solution composition, separate from the pure "solid vs. liquid" structural argument. In short: same fluorophore, same basic vibronic-progression mechanism, but expect broader, possibly somewhat shifted, and likely less intense/less cleanly resolved bands than in the solid hexahydrate studied directly in this experiment.

## Quick-Reference Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Jablonski diagram | State-and-transition energy-level diagram grouping electronic states by spin multiplicity (S = singlet, T = triplet) |
| Internal conversion (IC) | Fast, spin-allowed, non-radiative transition between states of the same multiplicity (energy → vibrations/heat) |
| Intersystem crossing (ISC) | Spin-forbidden, non-radiative transition between different multiplicities, enabled by spin–orbit coupling |
| Kasha's rule | Emission occurs from the lowest excited state of a given multiplicity (S₁ for fluorescence, T₁ for phosphorescence), regardless of initial excitation level |
| Franck–Condon principle | Electronic transitions are "vertical" (nuclei fixed) because nuclei are much heavier/slower than electrons; transition intensity ∝ vibrational wavefunction overlap |
| Born–Oppenheimer approximation | Electronic and nuclear motion are treated separately because electrons respond ~instantaneously to nuclear positions |
| Term value (T, G, F) | Energy expressed in cm⁻¹ (energy/hc); electronic (T_e), vibrational (G), rotational (F) contributions add linearly |
| Anharmonicity (χ_e) | Small correction accounting for the real (Morse-like) potential deviating from a perfect harmonic parabola; matters most at high v |
| Reduced mass (uranyl symmetric stretch) | ≈ mass of one oxygen atom, because U stays nearly stationary by symmetry in this particular normal mode |
| Backlash | Mechanical "play" in gears/screws that causes a lag when reversing direction; avoided by always scanning one way |
| Quantum yield (Φ) | Fraction of excited-state population that decays via a particular pathway (e.g., radiatively): Φ = k_i / Σk_j |
