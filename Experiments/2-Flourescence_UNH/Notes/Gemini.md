### 1. Molecular Photophysics & Transition Dynamics

Luminescence is the emission of optical radiation resulting from non-thermal electronic excitation. In uranyl nitrate hexahydrate, $\text{UO}_2(\text{NO}_3)_2\cdot 6\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (UNH), luminescence occurs through distinct electronic and vibronic processes.

```
                S₂ ═════════════════════
                   │   │   │
                   │   │   │ Vibrational Relaxation (10⁻¹³ - 10⁻¹¹ s)
                   │   ▼   ▼
                S₁ ═════════════════════ ──Intersystem──► T₁ ═══════════
                   │   │   │             Crossing (10⁻⁸ s)   │   │   │
      Absorption   │   │   │ Fluorescence                    │   │   │ Phosphorescence
    (10⁻¹⁵ s)      │   │   │ (10⁻⁹ - 10⁻⁷ s)                 │   │   │ (10⁻³ - 10 s)
                   │   ▼   ▼                                 ▼   ▼   ▼
                S₀ ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════

```

#### Electronic Spin States

* **Singlet State ($S$):** All electron spins are paired. Total spin quantum number $S_{spin} = 0$, giving a spin multiplicity $2S_{spin} + 1 = 1$. The ground state of UNH is $S_0$, and excited singlet states are denoted $S_1, S_2, \dots, S_n$.


* **Triplet State ($T$):** Two electrons possess parallel (unpaired) spins. Total spin quantum number $S_{spin} = 1$, giving a spin multiplicity $2S_{spin} + 1 = 3$. By Hund's rule of maximum multiplicity, the lowest triplet state $T_1$ lies lower in energy than the corresponding singlet state $S_1$ due to reduced electron-electron Coulombic repulsion.



#### Transition Mechanisms & Timescales

* **Optical Absorption ($S_0 + h\nu \to S_n$): $\sim 10^{-15}\text{ s}$ (1 femtosecond)**


Absorption is an electric dipole interaction between the electromagnetic wave's electric vector and the molecular transition dipole moment. It matches the timescale of an electron orbiting a nucleus ($\sim 10^{-16} - 10^{-15}\text{ s}$) and is governed by the **Franck-Condon Principle**: because nuclei are $\sim 10^3 - 10^5$ times heavier than electrons ($M_{nuc} \gg m_e$), nuclear motion is quasi-static during electronic excitation. Absorption transitions are therefore represented as vertical lines on potential energy diagrams.
* **Vibrational Relaxation / Thermal Conversion ($S_n(v) \to S_n(0)$): $\sim 10^{-13} - 10^{-11}\text{ s}$**


Molecules excited to higher vibrational levels rapidly lose excess vibrational energy to the surrounding crystal lattice via acoustic and optical phonon emission (lattice vibrations). This non-radiative dissipation occurs on the order of single molecular vibrational periods ($\sim 10^{-13}\text{ s}$).
* **Internal Conversion (IC, $S_n \to S_1$): $\sim 10^{-13} - 10^{-11}\text{ s}$; ($S_1 \to S_0$): $\sim 10^{-8} - 10^{-7}\text{ s}$**


IC is an isoenergetic, non-radiative transition between electronic states of the same spin multiplicity ($\Delta S_{spin} = 0$).
* *Why $S_n \to S_1$ is fast:* The energy gaps between higher excited states are small, creating high overlap (Franck-Condon factors) between isoenergetic vibronic wavefunctions. This explains **Kasha’s Rule**: photon emission almost exclusively originates from the lowest excited state ($S_1$ or $T_1$), regardless of excitation wavelength.
* *Why $S_1 \to S_0$ is slow:* The large energy gap between $S_1$ and $S_0$ requires converting substantial electronic energy into many high-order vibrational quanta simultaneously (Energy Gap Law), drastically reducing the transition rate.


* **Intersystem Crossing (ISC, $S_1 \rightsquigarrow T_1$): $\sim 10^{-8}\text{ s}$**


ISC is a non-radiative transition between states of different spin multiplicities ($\Delta S_{spin} \ne 0$). Although formal quantum mechanical selection rules forbid spin-flip transitions, ISC is enabled by **Spin-Orbit Coupling (SOC)**, where the electron spin magnetic moment couples to the magnetic field generated by orbital motion:



$$\hat{H}_{SO} = \frac{1}{2 m_e^2 c^2}\left(\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\right) \hat{L} \cdot \hat{S} \propto Z^4$$



In Uranyl compounds, the central Uranium atom possesses a high nuclear charge ($Z = 92$), creating a pronounced **Heavy-Atom Effect**. This strong spin-orbit coupling mixes singlet and triplet wavefunctions, increasing the ISC rate to $\sim 10^8\text{ s}^{-1}$.


* **Fluorescence ($S_1 \to S_0 + h\nu_F$): $\sim 10^{-9} - 10^{-7}\text{ s}$**


Fluorescence is a spin-allowed ($\Delta S_{spin} = 0$) radiative transition. The spontaneous emission rate is defined by Einstein's $A$-coefficient:



$$A_{21} = \frac{8\pi h \nu^3}{c^3} B_{21} \propto \nu^3 \vert{}\mu_{if}\vert{}^2$$



Because the density of electromagnetic modes is finite, spontaneous radiative decay is several orders of magnitude slower than vertical absorption ($10^{-9}\text{ s}$ vs $10^{-15}\text{ s}$).
* **Phosphorescence ($T_1 \to S_0 + h\nu_P$): $\sim 10^{-3} - 10\text{ s}$**


Phosphorescence is a radiative transition between states of different multiplicities ($T_1 \to S_0$, $\Delta S_{spin} = 1$). Because it is fundamentally spin-forbidden, the electronic transition dipole moment $\vert{}\mu_{T_1 \to S_0}\vert{}$ is very small, leading to long radiative lifetimes ($10^{-3}$ to $>1\text{ s}$).

### 2. Theoretical Derivations & Mathematical Formulations

#### Molecular Energy Breakdown

Under the **Born-Oppenheimer approximation**, nuclear and electronic motions are decoupled due to their mass disparity ($M_{nuc} \gg m_e$). The total molecular energy $E_T$ is separable into electronic ($E_e$), vibrational ($E_v$), and rotational ($E_r$) components:


$$E_T = E_e + E_v + E_r \tag{1}$$

Dividing Eq. (1) by $hc$ converts absolute energy into spectroscopic term values ($T$, in $\text{cm}^{-1}$):


$$T = \frac{E_T}{hc} = T_e + G(v) + F(J) \tag{2}$$

#### Rotational Term Quenching in Solid-State Spectra

In gas-phase spectra, isolated molecules rotate freely, yielding sharp, quantized rotational fine structures ($F(J) = B J(J+1)$). In solid uranyl nitrate hexahydrate crystals:

* Strong intermolecular crystalline electric fields create a deep potential barrier that quenches free molecular rotation.
* Rotational degrees of freedom collapse into low-frequency, hindered lattice librations/phonons that coalesce into broad continuous bands.
* Consequently, discrete rotational term values vanish ($F(J) \approx 0$):



$$T = T_e + G(v) \tag{3}$$




#### Anharmonicity and the Harmonic Approximation

The potential energy of a vibrating bond follows the Morse potential:


$$V(r) = D_e \left[1 - e^{-a(r - r_e)}\right]^2$$

Expanding $V(r)$ in a Taylor series yields the vibrational term value $G(v)$:


$$G(v) = \omega_e \left(v + \frac{1}{2}\right) - \omega_e x_e \left(v + \frac{1}{2}\right)^2 + \omega_e y_e \left(v + \frac{1}{2}\right)^3 - \dots$$


where $\omega_e$ is the harmonic vibrational frequency and $x_e$ is the dimensionless anharmonicity constant ($x_e \approx 0.001 - 0.01$).

* **Why anharmonicity can be neglected here:** Luminescence from UNH involves low vibrational ground-state levels ($v'' = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$). At the bottom of the potential well, atomic displacements from equilibrium are minimal ($\vert{}r - r_e\vert{} \ll r_e$), where the parabolic harmonic potential $\frac{1}{2} k (r - r_e)^2$ matches the Morse potential closely. Therefore, terms with $\omega_e x_e$ and higher powers contribute negligibly ($< 1\%$) to the energy difference and are omitted.



#### Derivation of the Vibronic Emission Spectrum

Following the Franck-Condon principle, absorption excites the molecule to an upper state. Fast vibrational relaxation ($10^{-11}\text{ s}$) de-excites it to the lowest vibrational level ($v' = 0$) of the excited electronic state before emission occurs (Kasha's rule).

```
  Excited State (T_el)
  v' = 0 ───────────────────────────────
            │        │        │        │
            │(0->0)  │(0->1)  │(0->2)  │(0->3)
            │        │        │        │
  Ground State (S₀)  │        │        │
  v'' = 3 ──┼────────┼────────┼────────▼──────── (Energy ~ 3 ħω)
  v'' = 2 ──┼────────┼────────▼───────────────── (Energy ~ 2 ħω)
  v'' = 1 ──┼────────▼────────────────────────── (Energy ~ 1 ħω)
  v'' = 0 ──▼─────────────────────────────────── (Energy ~ 0)

```

The wavenumber $\bar{\nu}$ of the transition from $v'=0$ of the excited state to $v''$ of the ground state is:


$$\bar{\nu} = T' - T'' = [T_e' + G'(0)] - [T_e'' + G''(v'')] \tag{4}$$

Substituting the harmonic approximation $G(v) = \omega_e (v + 1/2)$:


$$\bar{\nu} = (T_e' - T_e'') + \left[\frac{1}{2}\omega_e' - \omega_e''\left(v'' + \frac{1}{2}\right)\right]$$

$$\bar{\nu} = \underbrace{\left[(T_e' - T_e'') + \frac{1}{2}\left(\omega_e' - \omega_e''\right)\right]}_{\bar{\nu}_{00}} - v'' \omega_e''$$

$$\bar{\nu} = \bar{\nu}_{00} - v'' \omega_e'' \tag{6}$$

Here:

* $\bar{\nu}_{00}$ is the wavenumber of the electronic origin transition ($v'=0 \to v''=0$).


* The spacing between any two successive vibronic emission bands corresponds directly to the symmetric ground-state vibrational frequency:



$$\Delta\bar{\nu} = \bar{\nu}_{v''} - \bar{\nu}_{v''+1} = \omega_e'' = \nu_m \quad (\text{in cm}^{-1})$$




#### Uranyl Ion Dynamics and Reduced Mass

The uranyl ion $\text{UO}_2^{2+}$ is a linear, centrosymmetric triatomic molecule ($D_{\infty h}$ symmetry):


$$[\text{O}_{(1)} \xlongequal{\quad k \quad} \text{U} \xlongequal{\quad k \quad} \text{O}_{(2)}]^{2+}$$

It has $3N - 5 = 3(3) - 5 = 4$ normal modes of vibration:

1. **Symmetric Stretch ($\nu_1$, Raman/Luminescence active, $\sim 860\text{ cm}^{-1}$):** Oxygen atoms displace in equal and opposite directions ($\Delta x_1 = -\Delta x_2$), leaving the heavy central Uranium atom ($M_U = 238.03\text{ amu}$) stationary at the center of mass.
2. **Bending Mode ($\nu_2$, doubly degenerate, IR active, $\sim 210\text{ cm}^{-1}$):** Perpendicular displacement of Oxygen relative to Uranium.
3. **Asymmetric Stretch ($\nu_3$, IR active, $\sim 930\text{ cm}^{-1}$):** Oxygen atoms displace in phase while the Uranium atom moves in opposition to conserve the center of mass.

Because the central Uranium atom does not translate during the symmetric stretch mode ($\nu_1$), each Oxygen atom vibrates against an effectively infinite inertial mass. The secular equation for this normal coordinate reduces to single-atom motion:


$$m_O \ddot{x} + k x = 0 \implies \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m_O}}$$

Thus, the effective reduced mass $\mu$ is strictly the mass of a single oxygen atom ($m_O$), **not** the standard diatomic reduced mass formula:


$$\mu = m_O = 15.9994 \times 1.66054 \times 10^{-24}\text{ g} = 2.6568 \times 10^{-23}\text{ g} \quad (2.6568 \times 10^{-26}\text{ kg})$$

Using the relationship $\nu_m = \frac{1}{2\pi c} \sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}}$:


$$k = 4\pi^2 c^2 \mu (\Delta\bar{\nu})^2 \tag{7}$$

In CGS units:


$$k = 4 \pi^2 \times (2.9979 \times 10^{10}\text{ cm/s})^2 \times (16 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-24}\text{ g}) \times (\Delta\bar{\nu})^2 \approx 5.891 \times 10^{-2} \times (\Delta\bar{\nu})^2 \text{ dyne/cm}$$


*(To convert to SI: $1\text{ N/m} = 10^3\text{ dyne/cm}$)*.

#### Optical Train & Component Functions

* **UV Discharge Lamp:** A high-pressure mercury arc lamp that provides intense discrete emission lines in the UV region (predominantly $253.7\text{ nm}$ and $365.0\text{ nm}$). These photon energies ($3.4 - 4.9\text{ eV}$) exceed the electronic bandgap of UNH, driving the $S_0 \to S_n$ transition.


* **Parabolic Metallic Reflector:** The mercury discharge is placed at the focal point of a concave parabolic mirror. The mirror collimates omnidirectional emission into a parallel beam to maximize photon flux on the sample. A front-surface metallic aluminum coating is used because standard silver-backed glass absorbs short-wavelength UV ($\lambda < 350\text{ nm}$).


* **Focusing Lens:** A UV-grade fused quartz lens focuses excitation light onto the crystalline UNH sample. Quartz is used because crown/flint glass absorbs UV light.


* **$90^\circ$ Detection Geometry:** The collection axis of the monochromator is oriented perpendicular ($90^\circ$) to the incident excitation beam. This design prevents direct or specularly reflected excitation light from entering the entrance slit, minimizing stray light background and preventing PMT saturation.


* **Carl Zeiss Prism Monochromator:**
* Uses a Littrow/Czerny-Turner configuration: Entrance slit $\to$ Collimating mirror $\to$ Dispersing Quartz/Glass Prism $\to$ Plane mirror (doubling dispersion via double pass) $\to$ Focusing mirror $\to$ Exit slit.


* Rotating the calibrated drum alters the prism angle, scanning specific wavelengths across the exit slit.




* **Slit Width Configuration ($0.2 - 0.5\text{ mm}$):**
* *Trade-off:* Spectral resolution $\Delta\lambda$ is inversely proportional to slit width $w$ ($\Delta\lambda \propto w$), while light throughput scales quadratically ($I \propto w^2$).
* Slit widths $> 2\text{ mm}$ broaden spectral peaks and cause closely spaced vibronic lines to overlap. Excessively narrow slits ($< 0.05\text{ mm}$) reduce the signal-to-noise ratio below detectable limits. Settings between $0.2\text{ mm}$ and $0.5\text{ mm}$ optimize resolution and signal throughput.




* **Photomultiplier Tube (PMT):**
* Incident photons strike a photosensitive photocathode, releasing primary photoelectrons via the photoelectric effect.


* A series of $10-14$ dynodes, each held at successively higher potentials ($+50\text{ to }+100\text{ V}$), multiplies the electron cascade via secondary emission, producing an overall gain of $10^5 - 10^7$.


* The collected anode current ($10^{-9} - 10^{-6}\text{ A}$) is read directly by a high-sensitivity galvanometer or nanoammeter.


* *High-Voltage Bias:* Operated at $\sim 650\text{ V}$. Voltages exceeding $700-1000\text{ V}$ can cause Townsend avalanche breakdown, thermal dark-current runaway, or damage to dynode surfaces.

### 4. Data Processing & Error Analysis

#### Step 1: Calibration of the Monochromator

The dispersion of a prism is non-linear and governed by **Cauchy's dispersion formula**:


$$n(\lambda) = A + \frac{B}{\lambda^2} + \frac{C}{\lambda^4}$$

Because angular dispersion $\frac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = \frac{d\theta}{dn} \frac{dn}{d\lambda} \propto -\frac{2B}{\lambda^3}$, drum readings do not scale linearly with wavelength.

#### Step 2: Extracting UNH Fluorescence Peaks

1. Identify the drum readings ($D$) corresponding to the 4–5 prominent fluorescence emission peaks of UNH.


2. Convert each peak drum reading into its corresponding wavelength $\lambda_n$ (in nm) using the calibration curve.


3. Compute the corresponding wavenumber $\bar{\nu}_n$:



$$\bar{\nu}_n = \frac{1}{\lambda_n\text{ (cm)}} = \frac{10^7}{\lambda_n\text{ (nm)}} \quad (\text{in cm}^{-1})$$


4. Compute the successive vibronic separations:

$$\Delta\bar{\nu}_n = \bar{\nu}_n - \bar{\nu}_{n+1}$$


5. Calculate the mean vibrational frequency:

$$\langle\Delta\bar{\nu}\rangle = \omega_e'' = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N \Delta\bar{\nu}_n$$


6. Calculate the force constant $k$ using Eq. (7).



#### Error Propagation

The primary source of uncertainty in the force constant $k = 4\pi^2 c^2 \mu (\Delta\bar{\nu})^2$ comes from the wavenumber difference $\Delta\bar{\nu}$:


$$\ln k = \ln(4\pi^2 c^2 \mu) + 2 \ln(\Delta\bar{\nu})$$

Differentiating yields the relative uncertainty:


$$\frac{\delta k}{k} = 2 \frac{\delta(\Delta\bar{\nu})}{\Delta\bar{\nu}}$$

For two peaks located at $\bar{\nu}_1$ and $\bar{\nu}_2$:


$$\delta(\Delta\bar{\nu}) = \sqrt{(\delta\bar{\nu}_1)^2 + (\delta\bar{\nu}_2)^2}$$

Because $\bar{\nu} = 10^7 / \lambda$, the uncertainty in wavenumber relates to wavelength uncertainty by:


$$\delta\bar{\nu} = \left\vert{}-\frac{10^7}{\lambda^2}\right\vert{} \delta\lambda = \bar{\nu} \left(\frac{\delta\lambda}{\lambda}\right)$$

#### Major Sources of Experimental Error

* **Mechanical Backlash Error:** Gear play in the micrometer drum drive can lead to reading offsets depending on the direction of rotation. This is minimized by always turning the drum head monotonically in one direction (from higher to lower drum readings).


* **Finite Slit Width (Instrumental Broadening):** A slit width of $w = 0.5\text{ mm}$ corresponds to a finite spectral bandpass $\Delta\lambda \approx 1-3\text{ nm}$, which broadens peak profiles and introduces uncertainty when locating band maxima.


* **Calibration Curve Interpolation Error:** Using a second-order polynomial to approximate Cauchy's prism dispersion curve over a broad wavelength range can introduce systematic fitting errors.
* **Fluctuations in PMT High Voltage:** Because PMT gain scales as $G \propto V^{\alpha n}$ ($\alpha \approx 0.7-0.8$, $n \approx 10$, meaning $G \propto V^7$), small fluctuations in supply voltage produce noticeable changes in measured current, shifting apparent peak locations.

### 5. Solutions to Lab Manual Questions

#### Q1: Why do we use a metallic parabolic reflector in this experiment?

1. **Geometric Collimation:** Placing the UV source at the focal point of a parabolic mirror collimates omnidirectional emission into a parallel beam, maximizing photon density on the sample.


2. **UV Transmission/Reflectance:** Standard glass mirrors absorb UV light ($\lambda < 350\text{ nm}$) because of the electronic bandgap of $\text{SiO}_2$ and iron impurities. Front-surface metallic reflectors (e.g., polished aluminum) maintain high reflectance ($> 90\%$) deep into the ultraviolet range ($200-400\text{ nm}$).

#### Q2: What is the source of generation of UV light in the UV lamp in the experiment?

The UV source is a **high-pressure mercury vapor discharge tube**. An electrical potential accelerates free electrons through mercury vapor, exciting ground-state mercury atoms via inelastic collisions:


$$e^- + \text{Hg}(6^1S_0) \to \text{Hg}^*(6^3P_1 \text{ or } 6^1P_1) + e^-$$


Subsequent spontaneous radiative transitions back to the ground state emit characteristic ultraviolet resonance lines:

* $\text{Hg}^*(6^3P_1) \to \text{Hg}(6^1S_0) + h\nu \quad (\lambda = 253.7\text{ nm})$
* $\text{Hg}^*(6^1P_1) \to \text{Hg}(6^1S_0) + h\nu \quad (\lambda = 184.9\text{ nm})$
* High-pressure line broadening and transitions between higher excited states also produce the $365.0\text{ nm}$ (I-line) triplet.

#### Q3: Comment on the functional dependence of drum reading vs wavelength. Would it be the same or different if we were to use a grating for dispersion?

* **For a Prism (Current Setup):** The dispersion is non-linear. From Cauchy's relation ($n \approx A + B/\lambda^2$), prism dispersion scales as $\frac{dn}{d\lambda} \propto -\frac{1}{\lambda^3}$. Dispersion is high in the blue/UV region and falls off in the red region, producing a non-linear (cubic/polynomial) relationship between drum reading and wavelength.


* **For a Diffraction Grating:** The dispersion is linear. Governed by the grating equation $d(\sin\alpha + \sin\beta) = m\lambda$, the angular dispersion is:

$$\frac{d\beta}{d\lambda} = \frac{m}{d \cos\beta}$$



For small angles ($\cos\beta \approx 1$), $\frac{d\beta}{d\lambda}$ is constant, yielding a linear relationship between drum reading and wavelength across the entire spectral range.

#### Q4: What material is the optics made of? Why do we prefer fluorescence radiation in $90^\circ$ geometry?

* **Optical Material:** All optical elements (lenses, prism, cell windows) must be made of **UV-grade Fused Quartz / Silica ($\text{SiO}_2$)** or dense UV-transmitting crown glass. Standard borosilicate/soda-lime glass cuts off transmission below $350\text{ nm}$ due to bandgap absorption, blocking both the UV excitation beam and near-UV fluorescence.


* **$90^\circ$ Geometry:** Fluorescence is emitted isotropically ($4\pi$ steradians). Collecting emission at $90^\circ$ relative to the excitation beam prevents direct, unabsorbed excitation light and specular surface reflections from entering the monochromator. This reduces stray light, eliminates background artifacts, and protects the PMT photocathode from saturation.



#### Q5: How would the intensity of the fluorescence change if the temperature of the salt is increased or decreased?

* **When Temperature Increases:** Fluorescence intensity **decreases** (thermal quenching). Higher thermal energy populates higher phonon modes in the crystal lattice. This accelerates non-radiative internal conversion and intersystem crossing ($k_{nr} \propto e^{-\Delta E / k_B T}$), reducing the fluorescence quantum yield:

$$\Phi_F = \frac{k_r}{k_r + k_{nr}(T)}$$



Increased phonon coupling also broadens the vibronic peaks, lowering their resolved heights.
* **When Temperature Decreases (e.g., Liquid Nitrogen, 77 K):** Fluorescence intensity **increases significantly**, and the vibronic bands become narrower and sharper. Thermal non-radiative deactivation channels are frozen out ($k_{nr} \to 0$), raising the quantum yield close to its theoretical maximum ($\Phi_F \to 1$).

#### Q6: If the sample UNH is dissolved in water, will it still produce the same fluorescence spectrum?

**No, the spectrum changes noticeably:**

1. **Dynamic Solvent Quenching:** Water molecules act as strong quenchers through high-frequency $\text{O-H}$ stretching vibrations ($\sim 3600\text{ cm}^{-1}$). Non-radiative de-excitation increases via energy transfer into solvent vibrational overtones, reducing the luminescence intensity compared to the solid state.
2. **Equilibrium Speciation:** In aqueous solution, the uranyl ion hydrolyzes into various oligomeric species depending on concentration and pH (e.g., $\text{UO}_2\text{OH}^+$, $(\text{UO}_2)_2(\text{OH})_2^{2+}$, and $(\text{UO}_2)_3(\text{OH})_5^+$), each with its own characteristic emission profile.
3. **Loss of Lattice Field & Peak Broadening:** In liquid water, static crystalline coordination is replaced by a fluctuating solvent shell, broadening the sharp vibronic peaks observed in solid crystals into smooth, less-resolved bands.
4. **Vibrational Frequency Shift:** Coordination with $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ dipole ligands donates electron density into the Uranium $5f/6d$ orbitals, slightly weakening the axial $\text{U}=\text{O}$ bond. This shifts the symmetric stretching frequency $\nu_1$ downward by $\sim 10-30\text{ cm}^{-1}$ relative to the solid hexahydrate salt.
